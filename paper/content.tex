\begin{abstract}
Granular models relate aggregate variance to the concentration of
economic weights. This proportionality is exact only under restrictive
assumptions on marginal shock variances, dependence, and population
composition. Concentration is a level property, whereas variance
scaling is an elasticity defined along a population path. We show that
identical Herfindahl paths can generate different aggregate-variance
elasticities and derive an exact finite-interval decomposition of that
elasticity into changes in diagonal variance and signed covariance
components. The decomposition uses the logarithmic mean of total
variance and requires no logarithm of individual components. A
contribution-weighted average of component elasticities emerges only
under positivity and comparability conditions. Deterministic analytic
constructions verify the direct and reconstructed elasticities at
floating-point precision. The result clarifies the boundary of
concentration-based granular reasoning and supplies an accounting
framework for micro-to-macro analyses of diversification.
\end{abstract}%

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The granular approach restored an elementary but consequential fact to
macroeconomic aggregation: units enter an aggregate according to their
economic weights, so a small number of very large firms can prevent
idiosyncratic fluctuations from averaging out rapidly
\citep{gabaix2011}. Under mutually independent shocks with a common
variance, the variance of a weighted aggregate is proportional to the
Herfindahl index
\begin{equation}
    H(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i(N)^2.
\end{equation}
A fat-tailed firm-size distribution can therefore generate a
concentration path that declines slowly as the population expands.

This benchmark is sometimes given a stronger interpretation: that the
concentration path identifies the population-scaling rate of aggregate
variance. That conclusion is not general. Concentration describes the
distribution of weights at a given scale. Aggregate-variance scaling
is an elasticity describing how the complete variance--covariance
structure changes along a specified population path. The two objects
coincide only when restrictions on marginal variances, dependence, and
composition supply the missing bridge between them.

For the weighted aggregate
\begin{equation}
    X_t(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i(N)\varepsilon_{i,t}(N),
\end{equation}
aggregate variance is
\begin{align}
\label{eq:intro_variance}
    V(N)
    &=
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i(N)^2
    \operatorname{Var}\!\left[\varepsilon_{i,t}(N)\right]
    \nonumber\\
    &\quad+
    2\sum_{i<j}w_i(N)w_j(N)
    \operatorname{Cov}\!\left[
        \varepsilon_{i,t}(N),\varepsilon_{j,t}(N)
    \right].
\end{align}
The proportionality $V(N)=\sigma^2H(N)$ follows when all shocks share a
variance $\sigma^2$ that is stable along the population path and the
off-diagonal terms vanish. These are not incidental simplifications.
Even under independence, allowing the common shock variance to vary with
population scale gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:benchmark_elasticity_bridge}
    -\frac{d\log V(N)}{d\log N}
    =
    -\frac{d\log H(N)}{d\log N}
    -\frac{d\log \sigma^2(N)}{d\log N}.
\end{equation}
The concentration elasticity identifies the variance elasticity only
when the second term is zero. Heterogeneous firm-level variances replace
$H(N)$ by a variance-weighted concentration object, while common shocks
and economic linkages add off-diagonal terms. Population expansion may
also alter the size, sectoral, or network composition of the aggregate.
Once these channels are present, the Herfindahl index is not a sufficient
statistic for either aggregate variance or its population elasticity.

This paper separates the granular insight from that stronger inference.
Granularity correctly establishes that aggregate variance is weighted
by firm shares and may be dominated by a small number of large units. It
does not, by itself, establish that the firm-size distribution identifies
the rate at which aggregate variance changes with population size. We
make this distinction precise in two steps.

First, we show that identical concentration paths can generate different
aggregate-variance scaling. Equal-weight populations all satisfy
$H(N)=1/N$, but aggregate variance follows $1/N$ only under independence.
With persistent positive correlation, it instead approaches a positive
covariance floor and its scaling elasticity converges to zero. No
statistic constructed solely from the weights can distinguish the two
systems.

Second, we derive an exact finite-interval decomposition of the aggregate
variance elasticity into changes in diagonal variance and signed
covariance components. The formula does not take logarithms of individual
components and therefore remains well defined when covariance
contributions are negative. It also identifies the restrictive
positive-component case in which an aggregate elasticity may be read as
a contribution-weighted average of component elasticities.

The mathematical ingredients are established. Granular aggregation
studies unequal economic weights, dependence enters aggregate variance
through covariance, and logarithmic-mean methods provide exact additive
change accounting. Our claim is deliberately narrower than invention of
any of these ingredients. The contribution is their combination for a
distinct micro-to-macro object: the finite-interval elasticity of
aggregate variance with respect to population scale. This formulation
separates a level statement about weighted exposure from a path
statement about diversification, retains signed covariance
contributions, and yields an identity that can be checked directly in
empirical or synthetic data.

\section{Exact decomposition}
\label{sec:exact_decomposition}

Consider an aggregate composed of $Q$ contribution series,
\begin{equation}
    X_t(N)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}c_{q,t}(N),
\end{equation}
where $N>0$ indexes population scale. Its variance is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:variance_accounting}
    V(N)
    =
    \sum_{q=1}^{Q}\operatorname{Var}\!\left[c_{q,t}(N)\right]
    +
    2\sum_{q<r}\operatorname{Cov}\!\left[c_{q,t}(N),c_{r,t}(N)\right].
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ collect the diagonal and doubled
off-diagonal terms,
\begin{align}
    C_{qq}(N)
    &=
    \operatorname{Var}\!\left[c_{q,t}(N)\right], \\
    C_{qr}(N)
    &=
    2\operatorname{Cov}\!\left[c_{q,t}(N),c_{r,t}(N)\right],
    \qquad q<r.
\end{align}
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:component_sum}
    V(N)=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}C_k(N).
\end{equation}
Diagonal components are non-negative, whereas covariance components may
have either sign. The component universe $\mathcal{K}$ is assumed to
retain a comparable meaning at the population levels being contrasted.

For $N_0,N_1>0$, $N_0\neq N_1$, and positive aggregate variance at both
levels, define the finite-interval variance elasticity
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:direct_alpha}
    \widehat{\alpha}(N_0,N_1)
    =
    -\frac{
        \log V(N_1)-\log V(N_0)
    }{
        \log N_1-\log N_0
    }.
\end{equation}
For $a,b>0$, let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:log_mean}
    L(a,b)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \displaystyle\frac{a-b}{\log a-\log b}, & a\neq b,\\[1em]
        a, & a=b,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
be the logarithmic mean.

\begin{proposition}[Exact finite-interval decomposition]
\label{prop:exact_decomposition}
For each $k\in\mathcal{K}$, define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:component_alpha_contribution}
    \widehat{\alpha}^{\,C}_k(N_0,N_1)
    =
    -\frac{
        C_k(N_1)-C_k(N_0)
    }{
        L\!\left(V(N_1),V(N_0)\right)
        \left[\log N_1-\log N_0\right]
    }.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exact_alpha_decomposition}
    \widehat{\alpha}(N_0,N_1)
    =
    \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
    \widehat{\alpha}^{\,C}_k(N_0,N_1).
\end{equation}
The identity remains valid when individual covariance components are
negative.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:component_sum} implies
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
    \left[C_k(N_1)-C_k(N_0)\right]
    =V(N_1)-V(N_0).
\end{equation}
Summing equation~\eqref{eq:component_alpha_contribution} over $k$ and
using
\begin{equation}
    \frac{V(N_1)-V(N_0)}{L(V(N_1),V(N_0))}
    =\log V(N_1)-\log V(N_0)
\end{equation}
produces equation~\eqref{eq:direct_alpha} exactly.
\end{proof}

The decomposition attributes the aggregate elasticity to changes in the
underlying variance and covariance components without requiring their
individual logarithms. Signed covariance terms therefore enter directly,
provided total variance remains positive. The result is exact for each
finite interval and does not require a global power law. A regression
coefficient estimated over many population levels may still provide a
useful summary, but it averages interval-specific elasticities whose
component weights can change across the fitted range. The decomposition
therefore distinguishes an accounting identity from a parametric claim:
it reconstructs what drives the observed change in aggregate variance
without assuming that one invariant exponent governs every scale. In the
differentiable limit,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local_signed_decomposition}
    \alpha_{\mathrm{eff}}(N)
    =-
    \frac{d\log V(N)}{d\log N}
    =-
    \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
    \frac{N C_k'(N)}{V(N)}.
\end{equation}
If every component is positive, define
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_k(N)
    =
    -\frac{d\log C_k(N)}{d\log N}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weighted_local_alpha}
    \alpha_{\mathrm{eff}}(N)
    =
    \sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}}
    \frac{C_k(N)}{V(N)}\alpha_k(N).
\end{equation}
This resembles a weighted average, but its weights generally vary with
$N$. A fixed average of local exponents arises only under additional
restrictions, such as common component elasticities or contribution
shares that remain invariant along the population path. When positive
components decay at different power-law rates, their shares change with
$N$ and the slowest-decaying component eventually dominates. With signed
covariance components the normalized contributions need not be
non-negative, so the representation is not a convex combination and the
aggregate elasticity need not lie within the range of the diagonal
elasticities. Covariance is therefore not merely a correction to the
level of aggregate variance; its own scale dependence can determine the
limiting diversification rate.

Comparability across scales is substantive. If parts appear, disappear,
or change meaning between $N_0$ and $N_1$, the measured elasticity may
combine diversification with composition, partition, or dependence
changes. The identity remains valid only after those changes are
represented through a common accounting structure.

\section{Why concentration does not identify variance scaling}
\label{sec:concentration_scaling}

Consider the weighted aggregate
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weighted_aggregate}
    X_t(N)
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i(N)\varepsilon_{i,t}(N),
    \qquad
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}w_i(N)=1.
\end{equation}
For equal weights, $w_i(N)=1/N$, the concentration path is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:equal_weight_concentration}
    H(N)=\frac{1}{N}.
\end{equation}
Suppose first that shocks have unit variance and are mutually
independent. Aggregate variance is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:iid_variance}
    V_{\mathrm{iid}}(N)
    =
    \operatorname{Var}\!\left[
        \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i,t}
    \right]
    =\frac{1}{N},
\end{equation}
and its local variance elasticity is
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{\mathrm{iid}}(N)=1.
\end{equation}

Now retain the same weights and marginal variances but impose a common
pairwise correlation $\rho\in(0,1)$. Aggregate variance becomes
\begin{align}
\label{eq:correlated_variance}
    V_{\rho}(N)
    &=
    \frac{1}{N^2}\left[N+\rho N(N-1)\right]
    \nonumber\\
    &=
    \rho+\frac{1-\rho}{N},
\end{align}
with local elasticity
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:correlated_alpha}
    \alpha_{\rho}(N)
    =
    \frac{1-\rho}{\rho N+1-\rho}.
\end{equation}
The independent aggregate continues to diversify at rate $N^{-1}$,
whereas the correlated aggregate approaches the variance floor
$\lim_{N\to\infty}V_{\rho}(N)=\rho$ and
$\lim_{N\to\infty}\alpha_{\rho}(N)=0$.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=0.98\textwidth
    ]{figures/figure_1_concentration_is_not_scaling.png}
    \caption{
    Concentration and aggregate-variance scaling under equal weights
    and unit marginal shock variance. Panel~(a) compares the common
    concentration path $H(N)=1/N$ with aggregate variance under
    independence and under equicorrelation $\rho=0.15$. The independent
    variance coincides with $H(N)$, whereas the correlated variance
    approaches the floor $\rho$. Panel~(b) reports adjacent-interval
    variance elasticities: concentration and independent variance retain
    elasticity one, while the correlated variance elasticity falls
    toward zero.
    }
    \label{fig:concentration_scaling}
\end{figure*}

The counterexample establishes non-identification, not merely a
difference in variance levels. On the displayed grid, the correlated
adjacent-interval variance elasticity falls from approximately $0.500$
to $0.113$, while the concentration elasticity remains one. The two
systems have the same population, weights, Herfindahl index, effective
number of firms, and complete concentration path. What differs is the
population-indexed variance--covariance structure.

A fitted concentration law can therefore identify aggregate-variance
scaling only after marginal variance, dependence, and composition have
been shown to provide a scale-neutral bridge. This qualifies rather
than overturns the granular mechanism: unequal weights may remain
quantitatively important, but the firm-size distribution is not by
itself sufficient to identify the population elasticity of aggregate
variance.

\section{Diversification as a component balance}
\label{sec:computational_verification}

The independent homogeneous benchmark is a diagonal-dilution result.
For an equal-weight average of $N$ mutually independent unit-variance
contributions, aggregate variance falls as $1/N$. More generally, the
observed elasticity records how every source of variance changes along
the population path. A value below one need not represent one common
mechanism: it may arise because diagonal components decay at different
rates, because covariance offsets their decline, or because the
composition of the aggregate changes with $N$.

The first attribution example uses two independent positive components,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:heterogeneous_demo}
    V_{\mathrm{het}}(N)
    =
    N^{-0.9}+0.4N^{-0.3}.
\end{equation}
This example generalizes the common-exponent inheritance case. If all
positive components share the same elasticity and their populations
expand proportionally, the aggregate inherits that elasticity. When
component elasticities differ, however, no fixed population exponent
is primitive. The aggregate elasticity changes as the components'
variance shares change. In Figure~\ref{fig:alpha_verification}(a), the
fast-decaying component initially supplies most of the variance decline,
but the slow component becomes increasingly important; the aggregate
elasticity consequently falls from approximately $0.582$ to $0.422$.

The second attribution example groups the equicorrelated counterexample
into total diagonal variance and total doubled covariance,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:grouped_correlated_demo}
    D(N) &= \frac{1}{N}, \\
    B(N) &= \rho\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right), \\
    V_{\rho}(N) &= D(N)+B(N).
\end{align}
Although $B(N)$ is positive, it rises with population size. Hence
$B(N_1)-B(N_0)>0$ for $N_1>N_0$, and the minus sign in
equation~\eqref{eq:component_alpha_contribution} makes its contribution
to the variance-decay elasticity negative.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=0.98\textwidth
    ]{figures/figure_2_exact_component_attribution.png}
    \caption{
    Exact component attribution of finite-interval variance
    elasticities. Panel~(a) decomposes the heterogeneous independent
    aggregate into fast- and slow-decaying diagonal contributions.
    Panel~(b) decomposes the correlated equal-weight aggregate into
    diagonal and total doubled-covariance contributions. The positive
    covariance level increases along the population path and therefore
    contributes negatively to variance decay. Open reconstructed-total
    markers overlap the directly computed totals up to floating-point
    precision.
    }
    \label{fig:alpha_verification}
\end{figure*}

In this equal-weight case, the local decomposition has the especially
transparent form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diversification_balance}
    \alpha_V(N)
    =
    \frac{D(N)}{V(N)}
    -
    \frac{N B'(N)}{V(N)}.
\end{equation}
The first term records diagonal variance dilution; the second records
the signed effect of the changing covariance level. Independence gives
$B(N)=0$ and restores $\alpha_V(N)=1$. Under equicorrelation,
$B'(N)>0$, so covariance accumulation offsets diagonal diversification
and pushes the aggregate elasticity toward zero.

Equation~\eqref{eq:diversification_balance} gives a precise meaning to
delayed diversification. The scaling exponent is not itself a primitive
mechanism, nor is it a synonym for concentration. It is the net
accounting outcome of diagonal dilution and covariance accumulation
along the population path. Figure~\ref{fig:alpha_verification}(b)
displays this balance: the diagonal contribution is positive, while the
increasing positive covariance level makes a negative contribution to
variance decay.

The accompanying machine-readable artifact evaluates these identities
on $N\in\{4,8,16,32,64\}$. Direct and reconstructed elasticities agree
within the declared numerical tolerance; the maximum absolute residual
in the reference run is $6.7\times10^{-16}$. The calculations verify
the implementation of the accounting chain. Their substantive role is
to show that heterogeneous diagonal paths and signed covariance paths
can be attributed within one exact framework, not to provide empirical
evidence about a particular economy.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A concentration path describes how economic weight is distributed.
Aggregate-variance scaling describes how the complete
population-indexed quadratic form
$\mathbf{w}(N)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_{\varepsilon}(N)\mathbf{w}(N)$ changes.
The two coincide under homogeneous, independent, scale-invariant shocks.
Outside that model class, even the complete weight path does not
identify aggregate variance or its elasticity.

The decomposition developed here shows what replaces that
identification. An observed scaling exponent is the net outcome of the
population paths of all diagonal and covariance components. Common
component elasticities produce the familiar inheritance result;
heterogeneous elasticities generate changing contribution shares; and
covariance may reinforce or offset diagonal diversification. The
exponent is therefore an accounting summary of diversification, not a
primitive property of population size.

This changes the empirical question. Rather than inferring aggregate
scaling from concentration, one should define a coherent population
path, measure comparable variance and covariance components at its
endpoints, estimate the aggregate elasticity directly, and test whether
the component contributions reconstruct it. Concentration remains one
source of exposure. The rate at which aggregation succeeds or fails is
determined by how the entire variance--covariance structure evolves.
